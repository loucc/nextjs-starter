Many people start a diary and quietly abandon it — either they don't know what to write, or it turns into a log of events. An emotion journal is different: the goal isn't to record events, but to **understand feelings**. With one simple structure, five minutes is enough.

## The Four-Question Structure

Answer four questions each time you write:

1. **What happened?** — one or two factual sentences, no judgment ("My proposal was rejected in today's meeting")
2. **What do I feel?** — name the emotion: hurt, anger, loss, or even relief?
3. **What does my body feel?** — a tight chest, sore shoulders, a churning stomach? Emotions live in the body
4. **What do I need right now?** — not "how do I fix it", but "what do I need": acknowledgment, rest, or simply being heard

## Three Principles

- **No judgment** — this isn't an essay contest. There are no "wrong" feelings; writing them down is where acceptance begins
- **No schedule** — you don't have to write daily. Writing when emotions rise beats forced check-ins
- **No fixing required** — the journal's job is to *see*, not to *erase*. Many feelings begin to loosen the moment they're seen

## The Power of Looking Back

The most moving moment of an emotion journal isn't the day you write — it's the day you reread. The thing that broke you three weeks ago, now discussable in calm. The feeling of "this always happens", now visibly just twice. **Recording makes emotions traceable — and growth visible.**
